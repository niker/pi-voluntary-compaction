# Checkpointed context management and regular compaction: comparative results

## Abstract

This report captures the current state of the benchmark: **five regular-compaction runs (RC1–RC5)** and **thirteen checkpointed-context runs (VC1–VC13)** on one book, one task prompt, and one model. Mean composite score is **91.25** for RC (range 88.96–93.75) and **97.60** for VC (range 94.01–99.09), a descriptive difference of **+6.35 points**. The newest release, **1.0.15**, was run seven times (VC7–VC13): mean **97.72**, range 96.33–99.09, standard deviation 0.98. The two cohorts do not overlap — the best RC run (93.75) scores below the worst VC run (94.01) and below the worst 1.0.15 run (96.33). Modeled context cost is statistically indistinguishable between RC and 1.0.15 (14.24 vs 12.65 points, exact two-sided Mann-Whitney p = 0.76), while VC active time is 30% lower and wall time 53% lower, with a four-fold tighter cost spread.

## 1. Scope and method

Eighteen scored arms are included in the head-to-head comparison; three additional cloud-model arms that vary context-window size are reported separately in section 6 and are excluded from every figure below. All eighteen arms use the same task prompt (identical checksum), the same source book, and the same model identifier read from each session trace. The manipulated variable is the context strategy — regular compaction versus checkpointed context — and, inside VC, the release of the checkpoint extension under test: 1.0.0 (VC1–VC3), 1.0.10 (VC4), 1.0.11 (VC5), 1.0.13 (VC6), 1.0.15 (VC7–VC13). Versions are evaluated as recorded, on the first attempt made immediately before that version's public release; attempts that showed an obvious regression and prompted a code or instruction change are omitted, including one 1.0.15 attempt removed at operator order.

Scores come from frozen per-run `eval.json` and adjudicated `result.json`. Cohort summaries are regenerated by `bench/rollup.py`, `bench/build_composite.py` (`rc-composite.json`, `vc-composite.json`) and `bench/aggregate.py` (`comparison_results.json`, which now also stores per-release VC group means). The composite is the weighted mean of five components normalized by the configured weight sum 0.76: single needles (0.31), semantic multi-hop (0.15), structured chapters (0.10), structured characters (0.10), early retention (0.10). Chapter scoring credits four fields per chapter — Czech numeral, title, first text line, final text line — at 25% each, so a run that reproduces chapter boundaries but misses the closing line of every chapter still earns 75%. Character scoring is set F1 against a 46-person key. Early retention is derived from the early-position needle subset and is at ceiling in every arm.

### Summary of the score table

The separation is clean and larger than the within-cohort noise. RC mean 91.25 (SD 1.95) against 1.0.15 mean 97.72 (SD 0.98); every one of the seven newest-release runs exceeds every one of the five RC runs, which gives an exact two-sided Mann-Whitney p = 0.0025 and Cohen's d = 4.5 for that pair (p = 0.0002, d = 4.0 for all thirteen VC runs against RC). With n = 5 and n = 7 the direction and rough magnitude of the RC-versus-1.0.15 difference are established; the residual spread is run-to-run variation at fixed release.

Weighted by the scoring weights, the +6.47 gap between RC and 1.0.15 decomposes as: chapter fields **4.00 points (62%)**, character enumeration **1.48 (23%)**, single needles **0.79 (12%)**, multi-hop **0.19 (3%)**, early retention 0. In other words, under the current four-field chapter rule the RC deficit is dominated by reproducing chapter boundaries in full — chiefly the closing line — not by losing facts. Every VC run detected and reproduced all nineteen chapters; four of five RC runs found all nineteen but earned only 50–75% field credit, and RC3 missed one chapter boundary outright (72.97).

Three observations sit at the ceiling and should not be read as release effects: early retention is 100% in all eighteen arms, chapters are 100% in all thirteen VC arms, and needles are ≥95.59 everywhere. Within 1.0.15 the only component with material variance is characters (84.09 to 97.78); needles slip in two of seven runs and multi-hop drops to 90.62 in two. Character quality is therefore the axis on which the newest release still varies, and it is wide enough that the best RC run on characters (RC2, 89.41) beats the worst 1.0.15 run (VC9, 84.09).

Two release-level notes are worth recording without causal claims. First, the newest release is not the highest-scoring one: single-run arms on 1.0.11 (98.75) and 1.0.13 (98.78) sit above the 1.0.15 mean of 97.72, although each rests on one run only. Second, the 1.0.0 cohort is clearly the weakest VC cohort (96.32), and its weakness is concentrated in characters (81.81 mean; VC2 at 68.42 is the lowest character score in the benchmark) rather than in recall.

Wall and active time come from session traces; "active" excludes gaps longer than 60 s, so wall time is contaminated by operator absence (RC2 alone records 104.7 idle minutes of its 135-minute span) and the active column and modeled context points are the comparable measures. ### Summary of the cost table

Mean modeled context cost does not separate the cohorts: RC 14.24 (SD 4.10) versus 1.0.15 12.65 (SD 1.05), exact two-sided p = 0.76, d = −0.6. What separates them is variance and time. Every VC run lands between 9.33 and 13.96 points; RC ranges from 10.27 to 20.58, with a standard deviation four times the newest release's. VC active time is 30% lower than RC (17m09s versus 24m32s) and wall time 53% lower, and the VC idle column is roughly a third of RC's. The mechanism visible in the traces is structural rather than behavioral: RC sessions were interrupted 72 times in total by forced compaction (9–20 per run), which re-feeds summarized context, while VC sessions ran with zero forced compactions and 6–10 self-initiated checkpoint jumps each (100 jumps across the cohort). Higher tool counts on RC (83.4 versus 75.7 mean) point the same way — more recovery work per unit of answered question.

Run-level behaviour worth noting. **RC2** is the cost outlier on every axis: 20.58 points, 33.0 book-equivalents, 104.7 idle minutes and the lowest CPS in the benchmark (4.32) — its wall time should not be quoted as a model-speed figure. **RC3** did the most work of any arm (117 turns, 119 tool calls, 58.97 provider points) and still scored below the VC mean; it also logged ten failed tool results, the highest in the set. **RC4** is the cheapest RC arm (10.27 points, CPS 8.95, the only RC run in the top five of the efficiency ranking) while scoring 91.89 — cheap and mediocre, not cheap and clever. **RC5** read the least of any RC arm (10.0 book-equivalents) and paid for it with the cohort's lowest needles and characters.

Inside 1.0.15, effort does not track score: VC13 used the fewest turns of the cohort (63) and scored 98.86, while VC8 used the most (83) and scored 97.54. **VC13** combined the second-highest composite with the lowest retrieval volume of any arm in the benchmark (5.6 book-equivalents) and gives the best efficiency of the newest release (CPS 8.26). **VC10** reached the highest composite overall (99.09, characters 97.78) at the second-highest context cost of its release, so its CPS is near the bottom of the VC cohort (7.10) — thoroughness bought with context. **VC12** is the newest release's weakest run (96.33), and it also has the cohort's highest jump count (10), longest idle (22.2 min) and second-highest context cost (13.83), i.e. the low score came with the most churn rather than with less work; it is also the lowest CPS in the benchmark after RC2. **VC9** is the opposite pairing: shortest wall time (24m56s) and lowest idle (9.0 min) of any arm, good efficiency (CPS 8.82), but the release's lowest character score. **VC7**, the first 1.0.15 run, used the fewest tool calls of its release (66) and still ranks third on CPS within it.

Across older releases, **VC6** (1.0.13) is the most frugal arm in the whole benchmark — 4.1 book-equivalents, 51 tool calls, 9.33 points, CPS 10.59 — and **VC5** (1.0.11) is third on CPS among VC arms at 9.32; both rest on single runs. **VC1** shows that raw re-reading volume is not what drives modeled cost: it read 28.7 book-equivalents yet finished at 9.44 points and CPS 10.21, consistent with cache-friendly repeated access. **VC3** is the counter-example, reading only 6.3 book-equivalents but costing 13.94 points.

## 5. Interpretation and limitations

The general pattern is established by these samples: on this task, checkpointed context scores higher than regular compaction, with no overlap between cohorts, and it does so with less elapsed and active time and far less variance in modeled cost. The score advantage is concentrated in the components that require reproducing long structured outputs — chapter fields and the character roster — which is consistent with a strategy that preserves verbatim detail across context boundaries rather than one that replaces it with summaries; the traces support this only to the extent that RC sessions were repeatedly rewritten by forced compaction.

The release-level picture inside VC is weaker evidence. Older releases are represented by one run each (1.0.10, 1.0.11, 1.0.13) or three (1.0.0), so the apparent ranking of 1.0.11 and 1.0.13 above the 1.0.15 mean, and the efficiency advantage of 1.0.13, are single-run observations that the seven-run 1.0.15 spread cannot rule out as noise. Within 1.0.15 the remaining score variance is almost entirely the character component, and no run-to-run factor in the traces (turns, tool calls, jumps, retrieval volume) tracks it; attributing it to any specific release feature would be speculation.

Cost conclusions are bounded by the metrics. Modeled context points are a reconstruction from provider token totals with an inferred cached/uncached split, not observed cache telemetry; wall and active time include operator absence; retrieval volume is a trace estimate, not evidence of attention. The cohorts were not randomized or matched beyond identical prompt, book and model, and RC arms predate the extension, so part of any difference may sit in unrecorded harness changes rather than in checkpointing itself. These are stored observational runs: they support the ranking and the variance claim, and they do not by themselves establish a causal size for either.

## 6. Cloud model under artificially limited context windows (exploratory)

Three further arms run the same checkpointed harness against the same book on a hosted model (GPT-6-Luna) with its context window **artificially capped** at 48k, 72k and 128k tokens. Window size is the only intended variable. These arms are excluded from the cohort means above: they are a different model with different pricing, so their points are not comparable to Tables A and B, and their cost figures are only comparable to each other. One run per arm. `model-settings.json` in all three directories is stale — it records the local model and a 72,192-token window for every arm — so the window difference is established by the experiment setup rather than by recorded metadata, and the cost audit was re-run with explicit window overrides.

**Summary of the cloud table.** The response curve is non-monotonic on both axes: 72k takes the composite *and* every individual component against both neighbours, and it is also the cheapest and fastest arm. The shape, not the position of the optimum, is the finding.

- Growing the window from 72k to 128k raised cumulative input tokens by **68.7 %**, modeled context cost by **14.0 %** and wall time by **6.5 %**, while composite fell **6.16 points**. The additional headroom was consumed rather than banked: a larger window lets more material stay in flight per turn, and every re-sent prefix is billed. Nothing in the trace shows that extra material converted into recall — 128k actually lost the most on early-book retention, which is the opposite of the intuition that a small window forgets the opening.
- Shrinking the window from 72k to 48k cost **8.44 composite points** — more than the 128k arm lost by growing — and did so while becoming the *most* expensive arm on every cost metric (+26.7 % modeled context, +26 % wall). The mechanism is orchestration rather than tokens per turn: the 48k window forced 10 checkpoint jumps and 68 assistant turns against 5 and 47 at 72k, and re-read the book 3.37 times instead of 2.36, with each jump re-entering an uncached prefix. Cost therefore rises at both ends of the tested range, with a floor in the middle.
- The component shape suggests where pressure bites first: at 48k the losses concentrate in multi-hop synthesis (−21.87 against 72k) and roster coverage (−17.31), while short-fact recall holds up (+2.94 needles over 128k, early retention tied with 72k). Reading a fact survives compression; holding several facts together long enough to combine them does not.
- Several failures are shared by all three windows — the same chapter end-boundary convention, the same missing roster entry, the same refusal pattern on one needle family — so they are model-level rather than window-level, and no window setting in this range removes them.
- Limits: one run per arm, a small prompt-text difference between arms that the experiment owner instructed be ignored, and no cache telemetry behind the modeled split. A single sample per point cannot locate an optimum or support any numeric claim; it does motivate the question. On this task a larger window did not buy accuracy, it bought tokens and elapsed time, which is consistent with context-window size behaving as an **economic** variable rather than a capacity one above some threshold. Confirming that needs at least three runs per arm, an intermediate setting between 72k and 128k, and the same treatment under the compaction strategy — independent testing in this direction is welcome.
